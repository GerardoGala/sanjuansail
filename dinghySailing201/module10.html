Module 10

Performance

Bring everything together to sail a dinghy efficiently, smoothly, and with greater control.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Explain the factors that affect dinghy sailing performance.
  • Coordinate sail controls to match changing conditions.
  • Maintain effective boat balance and trim.
  • Use sailor position to improve boat performance.
  • Recognize the relationship between speed, steering, and sail trim.
  • Make small, deliberate adjustments rather than constantly making large changes.

What Is Sailing Performance?

Sailing performance is not simply about making the boat go as fast as possible.

Good performance means using the available wind efficiently while maintaining control, balance, and a safe margin.

A fast boat that is constantly out of balance, overpowered, or difficult to steer is not being sailed efficiently.

Sailing Is a System

The boat's performance is the result of several systems working together.

  • Wind: provides the energy.
  • Sail: converts wind energy into aerodynamic force.
  • Daggerboard: provides lateral resistance and reduces leeway.
  • Rudder: controls the boat's direction.
  • Sailor: controls the boat and helps maintain balance.

Performance comes from making all of these parts work together.

Start With Balance

A well-balanced boat is easier to steer and usually moves more efficiently through the water.

The sailor should continuously monitor heel and trim.

If the boat heels excessively, the sailor may need to change body position, ease the mainsheet, or adjust another control.

The goal is not necessarily to keep the boat perfectly upright at all times. The goal is to maintain the amount of heel that produces good control and performance for the boat and conditions.

Efficient Sail Trim

The sail should be adjusted so that it produces useful force without creating unnecessary drag or excessive heel.

The sailor can use:

  • Mainsheet to control boom angle.
  • Outhaul to control lower sail shape.
  • Vang to control leech tension and twist.
  • Downhaul to control luff tension and sail shape.

These controls should be adjusted together rather than treated as independent systems.

Sailor Position

The sailor's body position can have a major effect on performance.

Moving to windward can help counteract sail force and control heel.

Moving forward or aft can change the trim of the hull and influence how efficiently the boat moves through the water.

The sailor should move deliberately and smoothly.

Efficient Steering

Excessive rudder movement creates drag.

Good sailors therefore steer with small, smooth movements whenever possible.

The rudder should not constantly be correcting problems that could have been solved through better sail trim or sailor position.

A useful goal is to keep the rudder moving as little as necessary while maintaining the desired course.

Speed Is Not Everything

The fastest heading is not always the best heading.

When sailing toward a destination, the sailor must consider both speed and direction.

A boat traveling very fast in the wrong direction is not making good progress.

Performance therefore means balancing:

  • Boat speed
  • Course
  • Wind angle
  • Boat balance
  • Sail efficiency

Upwind Performance

When sailing upwind, the sailor generally wants the boat to point as high into the wind as practical while maintaining useful speed.

The daggerboard should provide sufficient lateral resistance to reduce leeway.

Sail trim and boat balance become especially important because upwind sailing produces significant sideways force.

Small changes in steering and sail trim can make a noticeable difference in performance.

Reaching Performance

Reaching often allows the boat to accelerate because the sail can be eased farther from the centerline.

The sailor should continue to monitor heel, sail shape, and wind changes.

The daggerboard may be partially raised when appropriate for the boat and conditions.

Downwind Performance

Downwind sailing requires different priorities.

The mainsail is generally eased significantly, and the daggerboard may be raised according to conditions.

The sailor should remain aware of the boom and the possibility of an accidental gybe.

Good downwind performance combines speed with careful control.

Small Adjustments

One of the most important intermediate sailing skills is learning to make small adjustments.

Instead of suddenly pulling the mainsheet several inches, moving across the boat, or changing course dramatically, make a small adjustment and observe the result.

Then adjust again if necessary.

This process develops a sailor's ability to feel the boat.

Learn to Feel the Boat

Experienced sailors receive information from the boat continuously.

They can feel changes in heel, acceleration, steering pressure, sail pressure, and the movement of the hull through the water.

These sensations provide information about what the boat needs.

The goal of intermediate sailing is to begin developing this awareness.

The Performance Loop

A useful way to think about performance is as a continuous cycle:

Observe → Adjust → Feel → Evaluate → Adjust Again

Sailing performance improves through continuous observation and small, purposeful adjustments.

Module 10: Performance & Winning Strategy

To win a race or beat a strict time limit (like the 180-second exam threshold), you cannot rely on "Base" control settings. You must actively optimize your sail shape, foil drag, and body mechanics for every distinct point of sail.

In a 15-knot breeze, an ILCA 7 is fully powered up. The objective is to make the right adjustment at the right time to maximize your Velocity Made Good (VMG). Use this master blueprint to configuration your boat for maximum speed.

1. Leg 1: Upwind Performance (Close-Hauled Beat)

The goal upwind is to de-power the sail just enough to keep the boat dead flat while sailing at an aggressive 45° angle to the wind. Excess heel creates massive rudder drag, killing your speed.

Control Element Optimal Setting Performance Impact & Logic
Mainsheet (Boom Angle) 0°-8° Block-to-block trim. Keep the boom on the centerline to achieve maximum height.
Daggerboard Down Fully lowered. Provides the maximum lateral resistance needed to stop sideways slippage.
Vang (Boom Vang) MAX Cranked hard. Bends the mast to flatten the center of the sail and restrains the boom from rising when you must ease the sheet in heavy gusts.
Downhaul (Cunningham) MAX Pulled tight. Moves the draft forward and opens up the top leech of the sail, allowing excess wind to spill harmlessly over the top.
Outhaul FLAT Pulled tight. flattens the bottom third of the sail to eliminate drag and reduce total overturning force.
Sailor Position Mid Hike Hard Sit in the middle of the cockpit longitudinally. Hike aggressively with straight legs to counter the 15-knot breeze and keep the mast perfectly vertical.
2. Leg 2: Reaching Performance (Beam to Broad Reach)

Turning corners shifts the boat into pure acceleration mode. Leaving upwind settings on a reach stalls the sail, drags the hull, and will trip the boat into an immediate capsize.

Control Element Optimal Setting Performance Impact & Logic
Mainsheet (Boom Angle) 35°-45° / 55°-65° Ease the sheet out until the sail is on the verge of luffing at the front edge, then trim in slightly. Keep it dynamic.
Daggerboard Center Raise the board halfway. As you head downwind, lift forces shift aft. Raising the board centers the lateral resistance and drastically eliminates underwater drag.
Vang (Boom Vang) CENTER Ease slightly from max. The top leech must twist open to lift the boom. If the vang is too tight, the boom will hit the water surface, tripping the boat over.
Downhaul (Cunningham) LUFF BASE Ease the downhaul. You no longer need to dump power over the top; easing it restores power and profile depth to the front of the sail.
Outhaul BASE Ease slightly to allow a slight curve in the bottom of the sail, adding aerodynamic chord depth for high-speed planing.
Sailor Position Aft Hike Hard Slide your weight toward the rear of the cockpit to lift the bow out of the water, preventing the boat from "pearling" (digging into the wave in front of you).
3. Leg 3: Downwind Performance (Dead Run Finish)

The final stretch requires absolute stability to avoid the "death roll." Aerodynamic drag is now your primary driver, so you want the sail to act like a giant, controlled parachute.

Control Element Optimal Setting Performance Impact & Logic
Mainsheet (Boom Angle) 75°-85° or 90° Square the sail entirely out perpendicular to the wind. Do not exceed 90° or the sail will back-wind and cause an instant windward capsize.
Daggerboard Up Raise the board up to 3/4 high. Tracking straight downwind requires almost zero lateral foil resistance. Leaving it down creates massive friction and penalizes speed by 30%.
Vang (Boom Vang) CENTER Pull the vang snug enough to stabilize the boom. This prevents the top of the sail from twisting forward over the bow, which is the direct cause of the unstable "death roll."
Downhaul (Cunningham) OFF Completely released. Let the luff go slack to project the maximum possible surface area to the wind pushing from behind.
Outhaul FULL Release to full depth. A deep, pocketed sail catches the most air column volume on a dead run.
Sailor Position Aft Center Sit well aft to keep the transom locked in and bow high. Avoid raw hiking; stay centered, using subtle body shifts to counter rolling rhythms caused by waves.
Pro Tip for the Exam: Prioritize your transitions! Adjust your daggerboard and vang before or during your turn at each mark. Waiting until you are already on the new leg means you have already lost valuable seconds and risked a capsize.

Key Takeaways

  • Performance is more than simply going fast.
  • A balanced boat is generally easier to control and sail efficiently.
  • Sail controls should work together.
  • Sailor position affects balance, trim, and performance.
  • Smooth steering reduces unnecessary drag.
  • Speed and direction must be considered together.
  • Different points of sail require different techniques.
  • Small, deliberate adjustments are usually better than large, sudden changes.
  • Good sailors continuously observe, adjust, feel, and evaluate.
  • Performance comes from making the wind, sail, boat, and sailor work together.
